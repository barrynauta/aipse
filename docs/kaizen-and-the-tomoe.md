# Kaizen, balanced by the tomoe

Most of AIpse helps it understand you: what happened, how it felt, what you value. None of that helps you grow. Growth is not a big plan. It is small things, done again and again, until they are just who you are.

Two old Japanese ideas describe it better than any app.

## Kaizen

**Kaizen** means "change for the better". In factories it became a management method, but its core is personal: small steps, all the time, too small to fail. Robert Maurer's *One Small Step Can Change Your Life* is the personal version: a step so small that fear does not wake up.

So once a week AIpse proposes one tiny step. Like any proposal, you accept it, change it, or skip it. The step is always something you do, never something about someone else.

A good step takes less than two minutes, or changes one thing you already do. "Train more" is not a step. "Put the gym bag by the door on Sunday night" is.

## The tomoe

The **tomoe** (*mitsudomoe*, with three) is the symbol of three drops swirling in a circle. Here, the three drops are **mind, spirit and body**.

From a distance they form a circle, and a circle is perfection. Look closer and they do not touch: each drop chases the one in front of it, and none of them ever catches up. So the tomoe is not perfection reached. It is perfection being sought, by keeping three things in motion and in balance. Heraclitus would have liked it: the balance is the movement, not a state.

| Drop | What shows it |
|---|---|
| Body | sleep, recovery, moving, training, food |
| Mind | learning and focus: practice, reading, new ideas |
| Spirit | mood, the journal, good moments, what the conscience held back |

The weekly step goes to the drop that lags. Each drop is measured against your own normal, never against a target or against someone else. Once a month, AIpse shows how the three drops moved:

> body: ahead. Trained twelve times, slept well most nights.
> mind: as usual. Language practice every day.
> spirit: behind. Two rough weeks, little journal.
>
> The drops are chasing: body is running, spirit is catching up.

## When a step does not stick

Stephen Covey (*The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, 1989) draws a habit as the place where three circles meet:

- **knowledge:** what to do, and why,
- **skill:** how to do it,
- **desire:** wanting to do it.

Miss one, and the habit does not form. A step that is skipped again and again is not a lazy step. One of the circles is missing.

AIpse already holds all three, in different places. The why is in your values and in each goal's "why it matters". The how is the step itself. The want is in the limbic layer: your mood, your energy, what you felt this week.

So the idea: when a step is skipped twice, AIpse does not push, and it does not just make the step smaller again. It asks which circle is missing.

- **No why:** "Does this still serve something you care about?" Maybe the goal behind it is quiet, or gone.
- **No how:** the step is too big or unclear. Make it smaller, or change one thing you already do.
- **No want:** "You have had a heavy week. Leave it for now?" A step can wait until the energy is back.

A nudge adds none of the three. Pushing harder mostly hurts the third circle, the wanting.

Covey adds a moral under his picture: give up what you want now for what you want later. That is the conscience's "not now", and it is useful. But a conscience that only works in one direction is just a nanny. So AIpse takes the three circles and leaves the sacrifice.

## What it is not

- **No streaks, no scores.** They turn growth into a game you can lose. A skipped step is simply skipped.
- **Balance, not maximum.** A month where all three drops move a little is better than a month where one runs far ahead.
- **The drops are not equal in size, and that is fine.** The tomoe is about motion, not about three identical drops.
